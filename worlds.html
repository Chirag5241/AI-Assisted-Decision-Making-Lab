Reading this bench

Hiding what the human misjudges trades early mistakes for a loss that never goes away.

Showing a misjudged feature costs regret now but lets the human learn it. The tables rank every subset by the proposal's objective, the discounted regret, so you can see which side of that trade wins for this world, learner and discount.

With several features the human is no longer simply right or wrong: whether the choice is best depends on the state. So instead of one flip round, each round is scored over 2,000 probe states drawn from N(0, I).

Actions work like features: dropping one the human misjudges removes those mistakes, but it can never be picked when it is the best move, and it is never learned.

Each table runs every subset as a fixed policy. The first varies the features and keeps the chosen actions; the second varies the actions and keeps the chosen features. Click a row to put that subset on the bench.

Fig. 2 is the one-feature beliefs chart, once per feature: each line is one action's weight on that feature, heading for its dashed truth. Faded lines belong to a hidden feature or an action that is not offered: they show how the weight would be learned if it were in use, while the regret figures treat it as never learned.

The value gap in Fig. 4 is maxk (Ux)k − û(ŷ), averaged over the probe states: above zero the human expects too little from their pick, below zero too much.

Fig. 1 follows one state, every feature at +1: the action the human picks there each round against the best move, with a grey box over the rounds where they differ. Other states can flip at other times, which is what the bar at the top and the lighter grey in the other figures add up.

Features here are independent, and every weight follows the chosen learning curve on the rounds it is shown.
