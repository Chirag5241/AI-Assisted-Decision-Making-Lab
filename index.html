Discount δ and horizon

δ is the algorithm's patience: one number in (0, 1), as in the proposal, Noti et al. and Guan et al. Near 0 only the first rounds count; near 1 later rounds count almost as much. It can also be read as the chance, δt, that round t happens at all.

Reading the bench

The more a wrong round costs, the sooner the human flips.

Time to flip moves inversely with the true gap |Δu|, and regret per wrong round is |Δu| · E|x|. The flip depends only on the ratio |Δh0| / |Δu|: for the hyperbolic learner it comes after half-life × |Δh0| / |Δu| rounds, exactly inversely proportional; for the other curves it still shrinks as |Δu| grows. Regret does not drive the learning: beliefs close the same share of their gap each round whatever the loss.

With one feature, the state x scales both actions alike, so the human is right exactly when h1 − h2 has the same sign as u1 − u2, whatever x turns out to be. Fig. 6 follows that gap.

When both actions share one learning curve φ, a human who starts on the wrong side flips as soon as

φ(t) > |Δh0| / (|Δh0| + |Δu|)

so the map in Fig. 4 is made of rays through the origin. Give each action its own speed and that picture breaks: the map then depends on the levels too, and a human who starts right can go wrong before coming back.

The regret loss of a round is maxk (Ux)k − (Ux)ŷ. Here that is |Δu| · |x| when the choice is wrong and zero when it is right; Fig. 2 averages it over x ~ N(0, 1) and weights round t by δt; the objective is the sum of those bars. For a negative state both the choice in Fig. 3 and the best move swap, so the regret is the same.

The value gap in Fig. 5 is maxk (Ux)k − û(ŷ): what the best move is truly worth minus what the human expects from the move they pick. Above zero the human expects too little, below zero too much. Averaged over states it equals (|Δu| − |h1 − h2|) · E|x| / 2, so it can sit at zero while the human still picks the wrong action.
