How to read the lab

Every bench simulates the same loop: an algorithm decides what a human sees, the human picks an action from beliefs that are partly wrong, and those beliefs move toward the truth with use. This page sets out the equations of that loop and the six figures every bench draws from them, so a figure means the same thing wherever it appears.

One round

  1. The algorithm chooses what is in play. It shows a set of features Ft and offers a set of actions At. This choice, over all the rounds, is the policy being tested.
  2. The human picks an action. The state of the round, xt, is drawn ({{ eq_link("value") }}). The human scores each offered action from the shown features with their own weights Ht and picks the highest ({{ eq_link("choice") }}). The round costs the regret of that pick at xt, against the best move there ({{ eq_link("regret") }}).
  3. The human learns. Every weight that was in use closes part of its gap to the truth ({{ eq_link("learning") }}). A weight that was held back does not move.

Conventions shared by every figure

  • Colour is the action. a1 is always the same blue, a2 the same orange, and so on, in every figure and on every slider. In Fig. 2 each feature's panel uses a slightly lighter shade.
  • Dashed in an action's colour is the truth: the true weight that action's solid line is heading for.
  • Grey behind a plot is error over all states. The darker the grey in a round, the larger the share of states in which the human would pick the wrong action. It is the same in every figure of a bench.
  • Solid is the draw, dashed ink its average. In Figs. 4 and 5 the solid line is taken at the state drawn in each round, and the dashed line is the same quantity averaged over all states.
  • Faded means out of use. A faded stretch of a line is a weight that was held back in those rounds, so nothing was learned about it.
  • Dotted vertical rules mark a change of policy: the rounds at which what is shown or offered changes.
  • Axes are fixed. In the standard figures an axis is set by the world, never by the data on it, so a figure keeps its scale while the policy and the learner change and heights can be compared by eye.
  • Rounds count from 0. A horizon of T rounds runs from round 0 to round T − 1.
