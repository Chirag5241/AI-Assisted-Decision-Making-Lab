Reading this bench

{% if aware %}

The algorithm shows the features from which the human gets this state right. Where the budget leaves none, no learning makes up for it.

Each round it looks for a subset of at most C features from which the human, with the beliefs they hold now, picks the best move, and shows the top-ranked one. So the human's choice in Fig. 3 is wrong only in the rounds where no such subset exists. Some states cannot be settled by any C features even by the truth: there a hidden feature is needed, and that share is a ceiling no belief can lift. The two-action problem writes the search out and lists what it leaves open.

{% elif timed %}

Holding something back for a while pauses what the human learns about it. It does not undo it.

Inside a window the feature or action is out of play: the human decides without it and its weights stop moving. When it comes back, each weight carries on from where it stopped. The tables set the schedule on the bench against every fixed subset under the proposal's objective, the discounted regret.

{% else %}

Hiding what the human misjudges trades early mistakes for a loss that never goes away.

Showing a misjudged feature costs regret now but lets the human learn it. The tables rank every subset by the proposal's objective, the discounted regret, so you can see which side of that trade wins for this world, learner and discount.

{% endif %}

Each round has its own state xt ~ N(0, I), and the regret of the round is taken there: zero if the human picks the best move at xt, otherwise what that mistake costs in that state. The large number is its discounted sum on this draw, and every row of the tables is run on the same draw. Draw the states again to see how much of a result is the draw.

With several features the human is no longer simply right or wrong: whether the choice is best depends on the state. The grey in the figures, the bar at the top and the “averaged over states” numbers therefore score each round over 2,000 probe states drawn from N(0, I).

{% if aware %}

A subset F works in round t when the best move a = argmaxk (Uxt)k ranks first on the shown features alone:

Σi ∈ F wai xi > Σi ∈ F wki xi for every k ≠ a, |F| ≤ C

The weights w are the human's own Ht, or the truth U, as chosen in the left panel. The margin of a subset is the left side minus the largest right side. When two moves are exactly equally good, either one counts as best.

Judged by the human's weights, a subset works when the human, as they are now, picks a from it. The algorithm shows such a subset whenever one exists, so the human's choice in Fig. 3 is wrong only in the rounds where none does: the grey boxes of Fig. 7. Among the subsets that work it prefers one on which the truth agrees, ranked by the truth's margin, so the pick stays right as the human learns.

Judged by the truth, a subset works when the hidden features are not needed to see that a is best. What is shown then does not depend on the human at all, and the human can still pick wrongly from it until they have learned those weights.

Either way the top-ranked subset is the one with the widest margin, or the one with the most features. When none works the algorithm shows one of the candidates at random. That is a stand-in, not meant to be good, until a better rule is decided.

The ceiling. With C below the number of features, some states have no subset that works even by the truth: the features that decide them cannot all be shown. The tile “States a subset can settle” gives the share that can. It depends only on U and C, and it is where a human who has fully learned ends up; only C = n makes it 100% for certain. While the human's beliefs still differ from the truth, those differences can tip a state either way, so early rounds can do better or worse than the ceiling.

The search is exponential. It tries every subset of at most C features: Σc ≤ C (n choose c) of them, which is 2n − 1 when C = n. With two moves it is not needed: rank the features by (wai − wki) xi and take the largest positive ones. With more moves, a subset has to beat every rival at once, which contains the hitting-set problem, so no shortcut is known in general. This bench has at most six features, so at most 63 subsets.

Fig. 7 counts the subsets that work in each round, out of all the candidates. A grey box marks a round where the count is zero and the random subset was shown.

What is shown decides what is learned: a shown feature's weights all move, for every action, and a hidden feature's do not. So on this bench the beliefs in Fig. 2 depend on the states that were drawn.

The table sets the policy against every fixed subset within the same budget, each kept every round, on the same draw. “Averaged over states” for the policy scores it afresh in each round with that round's beliefs: for every probe state it finds the subset the policy would show and lets the human choose from it.

Actions work like features: dropping one the human misjudges removes those mistakes, but it can never be picked when it is the best move, and it is never learned.

Every round must still show at least one feature and offer at least one action. The chips above the windows switch a feature or an action off for every round.

Each table runs every fixed subset, kept every round. The first varies the features and keeps the actions as scheduled on the bench; the second varies the actions and keeps the features as scheduled. When the bench's own windows change that family over the rounds, the bench is ranked as an extra row. Click a subset to put it on the bench in place of that family's windows.

Fig. 2 is the one-feature beliefs chart, once per feature: each line is one action's weight on that feature, heading for its dashed truth. A line is faded and flat across the rounds in which its feature is hidden or its action is not offered: nothing is learned about that weight then.

Dotted vertical rules mark the rounds at which what is shown or offered changes. “Once fully learned” takes the last round's choice and keeps it up until every weight in use has reached the truth.

The value gap in Fig. 5 is maxk (Uxt)k − û(ŷ) at each round's state: above zero the human expects too little from their pick, below zero too much.

Fig. 3 follows the drawn states: the action the human picks at xt against the best move there, with a grey box over the rounds where they differ. How often they would differ over all states is the bar at the top and the lighter grey in the other figures.

Features here are independent, and every weight follows the chosen learning curve on the rounds it is shown. Learning speed belongs to the feature: all features share one speed, or each has its own, and an action's weights on a feature always move at that feature's speed.
