How to read the lab

Every bench simulates the same loop: an algorithm decides what a human sees, the human picks an action from beliefs that are partly wrong, and those beliefs move toward the truth with use. This page sets out the equations of that loop and the six figures every bench draws from them, so a figure means the same thing wherever it appears.

One round

  1. The algorithm chooses what is in play. It shows a set of features Ft and offers a set of actions At. This choice, over all the rounds, is the policy being tested.
  2. The human picks an action. The state of the round, xt, is drawn (Eq. 1). The human scores each offered action from the shown features with their own weights Ht and picks the highest (Eq. 2). The round costs the regret of that pick at xt, against the best move there (Eq. 6).
  3. The human learns. Every weight that was in use closes part of its gap to the truth (Eq. 4). A weight that was held back does not move.

Equations

Numbered so an experiment can cite them. Each will link to its derivation once that is written.

Eq. 1

What an action is worth

(Uxt)k = Σj ukj (xt)j, xt ~ N(0, I)

Each round has its own state xt, one real number per feature, drawn afresh from a normal distribution (from N(0, Σ) on the Correlated features bench). The truth U has one row per action and one column per feature: ukj is the weight of feature j in the value of action k. A bench runs on one draw of the states x0 … xT−1 and can draw them again.

Eq. 2

The human's choice

ŷt = argmaxk ∈ At Σj ∈ Ft hkj,t (xt)j

In round t the algorithm shows the features Ft and offers the actions At. The human scores each offered action at the state xt with their own weights Ht, using only the shown features, and picks the highest. Actions that tie share the regret evenly.

Eq. 3

The best move

y*t = argmaxk (Uxt)k

The best move uses the truth, every feature and every action, whatever the algorithm holds back. Regret is always measured against it.

Drawn in Fig. 3, Fig. 6 Derivation not written yet

Eq. 4

How a belief moves

hkj,t = ukj + (hkj,0 − ukj) (1 − φj(Nkj(t)))

Nkj(t) counts the rounds before t in which feature j was shown and action k was offered. A weight moves only in those rounds; held back, it keeps its value and carries on later from where it stopped. The speed belongs to the feature: every action's weight on feature j follows the same curve φj.

Drawn in Fig. 2, Fig. 1 Derivation not written yet

Eq. 5

The learning curves

exponential φ(m) = 1 − (1 − r)m
hyperbolic φ(m) = m / (m + m½)
power law φ(m) = 1 − (1 + m)−a
sigmoid φ(m) = (s(m) − s(0)) / (1 − s(0))
with s(m) = 1 / (1 + e−5(m − m0) / m0)

φ(m) is the share of the gap between the first belief and the truth that is closed after m rounds in use: it starts at 0, never falls, and tends to 1. Each curve has one parameter, set on the bench: the rate r, the half-life m½, the exponent a, or the midpoint m0.

Drawn in Fig. 2 Derivation not written yet

Eq. 6

Regret loss of a round

ℓ(xt, ŷt) = maxk (Uxt)k − (Uxt)ŷt

What the best move is worth at the state xt of that round, minus what the chosen move is worth there. It is zero when the human picks the best move, and otherwise it depends on the state that was drawn: with one feature and two actions it is |Δu| · |xt| on a wrong round. This is the loss an algorithm would be trained on. Its average over all states, Ex[ℓ], is drawn beside it for reference: |Δu| √(2/π) on a wrong round with one feature, and the mean over 2,000 probe states on the other benches.

Drawn in Fig. 4 Derivation not written yet

Eq. 7

The objective: discounted regret

R = Σt = 0T − 1 δt ℓ(xt, ŷt)

The algorithm's patience δ is one number in (0, 1). Near 0 only the first rounds count; near 1 later rounds count almost as much. The sum is taken on the states that were drawn, so it changes from draw to draw. It is the quantity every ranking table sorts by, with every policy run on the same draw, and the tables give its average over states beside it.

Drawn in Fig. 4 Derivation not written yet

Eq. 8

Value gap

gt = maxk (Uxt)k − ût(ŷt), ût(ŷt) = maxk ∈ At Σj ∈ Ft hkj,t (xt)j

What the best move is truly worth at xt minus what the human expects from the move they pick. It measures how well calibrated the human is, not whether they choose well: it can be zero while the choice is wrong. Its average over states is drawn beside it.

Drawn in Fig. 5 Derivation not written yet

Eq. 9

Share of states with the best move

at = Px[ ŷt(x) = y*(x) ]

This one is not about the state that was drawn but about all of them: the share of states in which the human, with the beliefs of round t, would pick the best move. With several features that depends on the state. 1 − at is the grey behind every figure and the hatched bar at the top of a bench.

Drawn in Fig. 6 Derivation not written yet

Eq. 10

The flip, with one feature and two actions

right in round t ⇔ φ(t) > |Δh0| / (|Δh0| + |Δu|)

With one feature the state scales both actions alike, so the human is right in every state or in none: right exactly when h1 − h2 has the sign of Δu = u1 − u2. A human who starts on the wrong side flips once, when the curve passes this threshold. Δh0 is the first belief gap.

Derivation not written yet

Eq. 11

Filling in a hidden feature, when features are correlated

x̂ = PS x, E[xhidden | xS] = ΣhS ΣSS−1 xS

On the Correlated features bench the human predicts each hidden feature from the shown ones S and then chooses with the filled-in state, so they act as if their weights were Ht PS. A filled-in value never corrects the belief about that feature.

Conventions shared by every figure

  • Colour is the action. a1 is always the same blue, a2 the same orange, and so on, in every figure and on every slider. In Fig. 2 each feature's panel uses a slightly lighter shade.
  • Dashed in an action's colour is the truth: the true weight that action's solid line is heading for.
  • Grey behind a plot is error over all states. The darker the grey in a round, the larger the share of states in which the human would pick the wrong action. It is the same in every figure of a bench.
  • Solid is the draw, dashed ink its average. In Figs. 4 and 5 the solid line is taken at the state drawn in each round, and the dashed line is the same quantity averaged over all states.
  • Faded means out of use. A faded stretch of a line is a weight that was held back in those rounds, so nothing was learned about it.
  • Dotted vertical rules mark a change of policy: the rounds at which what is shown or offered changes.
  • Axes are fixed. In the standard figures an axis is set by the world, never by the data on it, so a figure keeps its scale while the policy and the learner change and heights can be compared by eye.
  • Rounds count from 0. A horizon of T rounds runs from round 0 to round T − 1.
