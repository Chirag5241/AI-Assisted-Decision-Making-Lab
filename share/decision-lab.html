Reading the bench

The more a wrong round costs, the sooner the human flips.

Time to flip moves inversely with the true gap |Δu|, and regret per wrong round is |Δu| · E|x|. The flip depends only on the ratio |Δh0| / |Δu|: for the hyperbolic learner it comes after half-life × |Δh0| / |Δu| rounds, exactly inversely proportional; for the other curves it still shrinks as |Δu| grows. Regret does not drive the learning: beliefs close the same share of their gap each round whatever the loss.

With one feature, the state x scales both actions alike, so the human is right exactly when h1 − h2 has the same sign as u1 − u2, whatever x turns out to be. Fig. 6 follows that gap.

When both actions share one learning curve φ, a human who starts on the wrong side flips as soon as

φ(t) > |Δh0| / (|Δh0| + |Δu|)

so the map in Fig. 4 is made of rays through the origin. Give each action its own speed and that picture breaks: the map then depends on the levels too, and a human who starts right can go wrong before coming back.

The regret loss of a round is maxk (Ux)k − (Ux)ŷ. Here that is |Δu| · |x| when the choice is wrong and zero when it is right; Fig. 2 averages it over x ~ N(0, 1) and weights round t by δt; the objective is the sum of those bars. For a negative state both the choice in Fig. 3 and the best move swap, so the regret is the same.

The value gap in Fig. 5 is maxk (Ux)k − û(ŷ): what the best move is truly worth minus what the human expects from the move they pick. Above zero the human expects too little, below zero too much. Averaged over states it equals (|Δu| − |h1 − h2|) · E|x| / 2, so it can sit at zero while the human still picks the wrong action.

Reading this bench

Hiding what the human misjudges trades early mistakes for a loss that never goes away.

Showing a misjudged feature costs regret now but lets the human learn it. The tables rank every subset by the proposal's objective, the discounted regret, so you can see which side of that trade wins for this world, learner and discount.

With several features the human is no longer simply right or wrong: whether the choice is best depends on the state. So instead of one flip round, each round is scored over 1,000 probe states drawn from N(0, I).

Actions work like features: dropping one the human misjudges removes those mistakes, but it can never be picked when it is the best move, and it is never learned.

Each table runs every subset as a fixed policy. The first varies the features and keeps the chosen actions; the second varies the actions and keeps the chosen features. Click a row to put that subset on the bench.

Fig. 2 is the one-feature beliefs chart, once per feature: each line is one action's weight on that feature, heading for its dashed truth. Faded lines belong to a hidden feature or an action that is not offered: they show how the weight would be learned if it were in use, while the regret figures treat it as never learned.

The value gap in Fig. 4 is maxk (Ux)k − û(ŷ), averaged over the probe states: above zero the human expects too little from their pick, below zero too much.

Fig. 1 follows one state, every feature at +1: the action the human picks there each round against the best move, with a grey box over the rounds where they differ. Other states can flip at other times, which is what the bar at the top and the lighter grey in the other figures add up.

Features here are independent, and every weight follows the chosen learning curve on the rounds it is shown.
