We now let the model come back. Availability is intermittent, the human keeps learning while alone ([[<G> - 7. Continued Human Learning]]), and when the model returns it plans from the human's **updated** state rather than resuming where it stopped. This removes the "omit future assistance" simplification of [[<G> - 6. Missing Assistance (Paused Learning)]].

Notation: $L(m)$, $R(F \mid s; p)$, $\kappa_d$ as in [[<G> - 6. Missing Assistance (Paused Learning)]]; solo map $G(m) = m + e_{F^*(p(m))}$ as in [[<G> - 7. Continued Human Learning]].

---

### 1. Model

* Availability $a_t$ is the Markov chain of note 6: stay $q$, return $r$, $a_1 = 1$. Now $r > 0$ is allowed and is planned for.
* Present round: $\mathcal{A}$ shows $F$; cost $\lambda R(F \mid s; p(m))$; $m \leftarrow m + e_F$.
* Absent round: the human reads $F^*(p(m))$; cost $L(m)$; $m \leftarrow G(m)$.
* With no fixed graduation time, every round is discounted the same way:
$$ \mathcal{L}(\pi) := \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t \ge 1} \delta^{t-1}\big(a_t\, \lambda\, \ell_{\text{reg}}(x^{(t)}, \hat{y}_t) + (1 - a_t)\, \ell_{\text{grad}}(x^{(t)}; p(m_{t-1}))\big)\Big]. $$
  (Note 6 used the verified convention — training rounds undiscounted, graduation tail discounted from $\tau$. With $r = 0$ the two agree after replacing survival $q$ by $\delta q$; see Corollary 2.)

**What the returning model needs to know.** After an absence of $k$ rounds that began at counts $m$, the human is at $G^k(m)$. Because $K = 2$ test selection ignores $s$ ([[2. Test Selection]]), $G$ is deterministic, so **the model needs only $k$** — not the cases seen or the human's choices — to reconstruct the human's beliefs exactly. (For $K \ge 3$ the human's choice depends on $s$, so the solo path is random and the model would need the human's solo history or a posterior over it.)

### 2. Bellman equations

Let $V_1(m, s)$ be the optimal cost-to-go when the model is present and sees $s$, $\bar{V}_1(m) := \mathbb{E}_s V_1(m, s)$, and $V_0(m)$ the cost-to-go of an absent round at counts $m$:
$$ V_1(m, s) = \min_{|F| \le c}\Big\{\lambda R(F \mid s; p(m)) + \delta\big[q\, \bar{V}_1(m + e_F) + (1 - q)\, V_0(m + e_F)\big]\Big\}, $$
$$ V_0(m) = L(m) + \delta\big[r\, \bar{V}_1(G(m)) + (1 - r)\, V_0(G(m))\big]. $$
Unrolling the second equation over the absence length:
$$ V_0(m) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \big(\delta(1-r)\big)^k \Big[L\big(G^k(m)\big) + \delta r\, \bar{V}_1\big(G^{k+1}(m)\big)\Big]. $$
Both are fixed points of $\delta$-contractions (costs are bounded by $2\kappa_d$), so they exist and are unique; the optimal policy is Markov in $(m, s)$.

**Adapting on return is automatic, and it can only help.** The optimal policy is a function of the current counts, so on return it acts at $G^k(m)$. A model that instead resumes a schedule planned at departure is one particular non-Markov policy, so its cost is $\ge \bar{V}_1$. What adaptation buys is explained by §4.

---

### 3. Special cases

**Corollary 1 (Paused learning: return is a reweighting).** If $G = \mathrm{id}$, then $V_0(m) = \dfrac{L(m) + \delta r\, \bar{V}_1(m)}{1 - \delta(1-r)}$ and
$$ V_1(m, s) = \min_{|F| \le c}\Big\{\lambda R(F \mid s; p(m)) + w_r\, L(m + e_F) + \beta_r\, \bar{V}_1(m + e_F)\Big\}, $$
$$ w_r := \frac{\delta(1-q)}{1 - \delta(1-r)}, \qquad \beta_r := \delta q + \frac{\delta^2 (1-q)\, r}{1 - \delta(1-r)} < \delta. $$

**Proof.** With $G = \mathrm{id}$ the $V_0$ equation is linear in $V_0(m)$; solve it and substitute. $\beta_r < \delta$ because $\delta r < 1 - \delta(1-r)$. $\blacksquare$

This is the equation of note 6 with weights $(w_r, \beta_r)$ instead of $(w_0, \beta_0) = \big(\tfrac{\delta(1-q)}{1-\delta}, \delta q\big)$. Planning as if the model never returns **over-weights** the graduation loss ($w_0 \ge w_r$) and **under-weights** the future with the model ($\beta_0 \le \beta_r$); equality iff $r = 0$. (Checked numerically against the two-equation system: agreement to $10^{-15}$.)

**Corollary 2 (No return: note 7).** If $r = 0$, $V_0(m) = W(m) = \sum_k \delta^k L(G^k(m))$ and
$$ V_1(m, s) = \min_{|F| \le c}\Big\{\lambda R(F \mid s; p(m)) + \delta(1-q)\, W(m + e_F) + \delta q\, \bar{V}_1(m + e_F)\Big\}. $$

**Commutation.** If showing $F$ does not change what the human would read alone, $F^*(p(m + e_F)) = F^*(p(m))$, then $G(m + e_F) = G(m) + e_F$: teaching what the human would study anyway moves an exposure earlier but leaves the solo path's choices unchanged.

### 4. Division of labour (independent case: $B = 0$, $\Sigma$ diagonal, $c = 1$, $\psi \downarrow 0$)

Use $\sigma_j = \Sigma_{jj}d_j^2$, $S_j(m) = \Sigma_{jj}p_j(m_j)^2$, $j^* = \arg\max_j \sigma_j$ (assumed unique) and $L_{\min} = \kappa_d(1 - \sqrt{\sigma_{j^*}/d^\top\Sigma d})$ from notes 6–7.

**Definition (handoff-safe).** Counts $m$ are handoff-safe if $p_{j^*}(m)\, d_{j^*} > 0$ and
$$ \Sigma_{j^*j^*} \min\big(p_{j^*}(m)^2, d_{j^*}^2\big) \;>\; \max_{i \ne j^*} \Sigma_{ii} \max\big(p_i(m)^2, d_i^2\big). $$

**Proposition 5 (Handoff).** From a handoff-safe $m$, under **any** later sequence of exposures (model or solo), the human always reads $j^*$ with the right sign. Hence every absent round costs $L_{\min}$, showing $j^*$ costs $\lambda L_{\min}$ per present round, and "always show $j^*$" is optimal; $\bar{V}_1(m), V_0(m)$ are then the constants solving
$$ \bar{V}_1 = \lambda L_{\min} + \delta\big[q\,\bar{V}_1 + (1-q)\,V_0\big], \qquad V_0 = L_{\min} + \delta\big[r\,\bar{V}_1 + (1-r)\,V_0\big]. $$

**Proof.** By the segment fact of note 7, along any future $|p_{j^*}| \ge \min(|p_{j^*}(m)|, |d_{j^*}|)$ with its sign kept, and $|p_i| \le \max(|p_i(m)|, |d_i|)$ for $i \ne j^*$. So $S_{j^*}$ stays above every $S_i$ and the human reads $j^*$, right-signed: $L = L_{\min}$ by Lemma 1 of note 6. In a present round any $F = \{j\}$ costs $\lambda \kappa_d(1 - \operatorname{sgn}(p_j d_j)\sqrt{\sigma_j/d^\top\Sigma d}) \ge \lambda L_{\min}$, attained by $j^*$. Every per-round cost is at its minimum, so no policy does better. $\blacksquare$

So in this case the model's whole job is to bring the human into the handoff-safe set, paying $\lambda R$ on its own rounds and $L$ on the human's. Notes 6–7 split that job:

* **What solo learning does for free (but at a price):** a salient distraction extinguishes itself (Proposition 2), and a salient misunderstood feature can correct itself (Proposition 3). The price is $L = \kappa_d$ (distraction) or up to $(1 + \sqrt{\sigma_j/d^\top\Sigma d})\kappa_d$ (wrong-signed $j$) per absent round while it happens. Whether the model should spend its own $\lambda$-priced exposures on these instead is exactly the trade-off the Bellman equation of §2 settles; it depends on $\lambda$ and on how likely and how long absences are ($q$, $r$).
* **What only the model can do:** a blind spot (Proposition 4) — a feature that matters more than the human thinks, never read alone. Exposure by the model is the only way its salience rises. Once raised past the competition with the right sign, the human finishes calibrating it themselves.

This is why returning assistance must re-plan from $G^k(m)$: during the absence the human may have extinguished the distraction the model was about to drill (those exposures are now wasted), or flipped a misunderstood feature (now it can be shown at regret below $\kappa_d$), or — on the non-monotone path of note 7 — be reading a wrong-signed feature, in which case the model's best first move may be to shield rather than teach.

### 5. Open

* A closed-form or index policy for §2 in general $(B, \Sigma, c)$; the myopic rule of note 6 with $W$ or $V_0$ in place of $\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}L$ is the natural heuristic, untested.
* Availability that depends on the human's state (the model is called back when the human struggles): then $r = r(m)$ and Corollary 1 no longer reduces to fixed weights.
* $K \ge 3$: solo choices depend on $s$, so $G$ is random and the returning model must estimate the human's state.
